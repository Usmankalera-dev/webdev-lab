What does semantic mean?

A semantic tag is one whose name says what the content is, not just how it looks. <div> says nothing — it is a generic box. <nav> says "this is navigation". <article> says "this is a self-contained piece of content". The browser shows them all the same by default. The difference is for everyone else who reads your page: screen readers, search engines, and other developers.

Think of it like labelling boxes when you move house. A box marked "kitchen" is useful. A box marked "stuff" is not. Semantic tags are labels on your page. A blind visitor using a screen reader can jump straight to the <main> content and skip the navigation. Google understands that the text inside <article> is the real content of the page, and ranks it better. And when you come back to your own code in six months, <footer> tells you instantly what that part is.

The main page tags

Almost every page on the web is built from the same five tags:

<header>  <!-- top of the page: logo, site title -->
<nav>     <!-- navigation links -->
<main>    <!-- the main content — only one per page -->
<footer>  <!-- bottom of the page: copyright, links -->

The <header> usually holds the logo and the site name. The <nav> holds the navigation links — it often sits inside the header, but it can also stand on its own. The <main> holds the content that makes this page different from every other page on the site. There must be exactly one <main> per page. The <footer> holds the copyright line, contact links and anything else that belongs at the bottom.

Notice that none of these tags does any styling. They are structure, not appearance. CSS decides how they look — you will style them in the next module.

Sections and articles

Inside <main>, two more tags organise the content. A <section> groups related content under one heading — a chapter of the page. An <article> is a piece of content that makes sense on its own: a blog post, a news story, a product card. An article can live inside a section, and a section can live inside an article — the rule is about meaning, not nesting.

Two smaller tags complete the set. <aside> is for content related to the page but not part of the main flow — a sidebar, a pull-quote, a box of related links. <figure> wraps an image (or a diagram) together with its caption in <figcaption>:

<figure>
  <img src="hunza.jpg" alt="Mountains in Hunza valley">
  <figcaption>Hunza valley in autumn.</figcaption>
</figure>

That is better than a bare <img> followed by a paragraph, because the caption is now clearly attached to the picture — for the browser, for screen readers, and for you.

Building a page skeleton

Here is the skeleton of a complete page, using everything in this lesson. Read it top to bottom — you can already see the shape of the whole site:

<!DOCTYPE html>
<html>
<head>
  <title>Ali's Bakery</title>
</head>
<body>
  <header>
    <h1>Ali's Bakery</h1>
    <nav>
      <a href="index.html">Home</a>
      <a href="menu.html">Menu</a>
      <a href="contact.html">Contact</a>
    </nav>
  </header>
  <main>
    <section>
      <h2>Fresh every morning</h2>
      <p>Bread, cakes and biscuits, baked daily.</p>
    </section>
    <article>
      <h2>Today's special</h2>
      <p>Chocolate cake — Rs 500 a slice.</p>
    </article>
  </main>
  <footer>
    <p>Ali's Bakery, Lahore — since 2015.</p>
  </footer>
</body>
</html>

That is the end of the HTML module. You now know the structure of every web page: headings, text, links, images, lists, tables, forms and semantic sections. From the next lesson, you learn to make it beautiful with CSS.

Exercise — Rebuild your page semantically

Take your personal page from the earlier lessons and rebuild its structure with semantic tags: wrap the top in a header with a nav, the middle in main with one or two sections, and the bottom in a footer. Wrap your photo in a figure with a figcaption. The page should look exactly the same as before — semantic tags change the meaning, not the appearance.
